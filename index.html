After the Shaking

Haiti's 2010 earthquake and the work of resilience
A visual narrative about emergency response, displacement, local action, everyday life, and long-term rebuilding.
Archival photographs with captions and source credits

On January 12, 2010, a shallow magnitude-7.0 earthquake struck near Léogâne, west of Port-au-Prince. The earthquake severely damaged buildings and disrupted homes, health care, transportation, and schools. Published death tolls differ by source, so this essay focuses on documented scenes and the work that followed rather than treating one disputed number as final. The photographs begin with rescue and emergency care, then move through displacement, aid, local action, everyday life, and rebuilding.

Resilience is a process, not an ending

Together, these images show that the earthquake was not one moment. It was an emergency that continued through overcrowded medical care and temporary housing, and then became a long effort to restore community life. International responders and relief supplies mattered, but so did Haitian residents clearing rubble, adapting shelters, caring for families, and protecting children's routines. The photographs also have limits: many in this sequence come from U.S. Navy archives, so the camera often belongs to an outside institution. A fuller history should include more Haitian photographers' work and survivors' own words. Resilience should be respected without being used to excuse slow recovery or unmet needs.
